Orbital Weirdness Atlas

I built a reproducible R pipeline to retrieve, quality-screen, rank, and visualize approximately 1.55 million objects from NASA/JPL’s Small-Body Database. The project asks whether “departure from a circular, coplanar, prograde orbit” can be summarized in a useful, interpretable number.

The question

Can a compact geometric index identify unusual small-body orbits, reveal meaningful differences among established orbital classes, and surface individual objects worth further investigation?

The reference state is the dynamically ordinary Solar-System geometry: a circular orbit in the ecliptic plane moving prograde.

The metric

Wvisible = √[ e² + (2 sin(i/2))² ]

e is eccentricity and i is inclination. The metric is zero for a circular, coplanar, prograde orbit and increases with eccentricity, plane tilt, and retrograde orientation.

HKL = √(1 − e²) cos i

I retained the signed Kozai–Lidov angular-momentum-like quantity as a physically meaningful comparator rather than forcing it into a second arbitrary score.

Pipeline and quality controls

Data engineering

  • Paginated full-population download from the JPL SBDB Query API.
  • Timestamped local snapshots to prevent a changing live database from silently altering results.
  • Resumable Parquet storage with automatic compression-codec fallback.
  • Schema normalization for identifiers that alternate between numeric-looking and textual values.

Scientific safeguards

  • Restricted the primary study to bound asteroid orbits with 0 ≤ e < 1.
  • Compared the full sample with a condition-code ≤ 5 quality screen.
  • Propagated reported σe and σi into approximate metric uncertainty.
  • Used full populations for statistics and class-stratified samples only for plotting.

Result 1: the index recovers interpretable population structure

The metric does not merely produce a leaderboard. Known populations occupy distinct distributions: Apollos and Centaurs have high central Wvisible; the main belt is concentrated near low values; TNOs have a modest median but an enormous extreme tail.

Result 2: class-relative scoring finds different kinds of outlier

Global ranks mainly return classes that are already geometrically extreme. I therefore added within-class percentile, robust-MAD z-score, and empirical rank surprisal. This surfaces objects that are unusual for their own peers.

Result 3: inclination dominates the extreme within-class tails

Ordinary near-Earth classes are often eccentricity-dominated. Yet among the top 1% inside nearly every class, the inclination term becomes dominant. For Jupiter Trojans and inner-main-belt objects, the weirdest tail is more than 96% inclination-driven on average.

Result 4: the leaders are not simply uncertainty artifacts

Most extreme, quality-screened objects have metric uncertainties many orders of magnitude below their Wvisible values. Exact leaderboards do change when weak orbit solutions are removed, but the broader population signal remains.

Important interpretation: Jaccard overlap in the original stability figure is depressed when quality screening changes the size of a class. Directional retention is therefore more informative: every screened Apollo, Amor, Aten, Centaur, TNO, and Trojan leader was already present in the corresponding unrestricted leader set.

Result 5: a useful negative result

Wvisible and signed HKL rank the observed population almost identically. For the dense low-e, low-i population,

HKL ≈ 1 − Wvisible² / 2

The analysis therefore rejected the idea that these were two independent weirdness dimensions. Wvisible remains the more intuitive descriptive score; HKL contributes physical interpretation and preserves the prograde/retrograde sign.

What the project demonstrates

Technical skills

  • R, tidyverse, ggplot2, Arrow/Parquet, API ingestion
  • large-data workflow design and failure recovery
  • robust rankings, enrichment analysis, information measures
  • uncertainty propagation and sensitivity analysis
  • publication-style visualization and scientific communication

Research judgment

  • distinguishing global from class-relative anomaly detection
  • recognizing selection effects and missing physical-property data
  • separating descriptive geometry from dynamical interpretation
  • reporting a strong null/redundancy result
  • designing a clear route from exploratory analysis to dynamical validation

Limitations and next experiments

Current claim: Wvisible is an interpretable descriptive index of orbital geometry. It is not yet established as a predictor of long-term dynamical instability.
  • Benchmark against eccentricity alone, inclination alone, Mahalanobis distance, density-based anomaly scores, and established orbital-similarity criteria.
  • Add a direction-blind plane metric, Wplane, to separate geometric plane tilt from retrograde motion.
  • Retrieve full covariance matrices for top-ranked objects and Monte Carlo their rank uncertainty.
  • Integrate high-, median-, and low-W objects with an N-body code to test whether class-relative weirdness predicts encounters, resonance changes, or survival time.
